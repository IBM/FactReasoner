# Annotator brief: logical coherence of long-form answers

Thank you for taking part. The session takes **at most two hours**, in one sitting with one
break. Work alone. Until you have sent back your exports, do not open the study data, the
paper, any scores, or another annotator's files, and do not discuss items with anyone.

## What you judge

An answer makes **claims**, and it draws **relations** between them. It gives one claim as a
reason for another, says two claims cannot both hold, or says one claim holds exactly when
another does. Every claim has a **truth value**, shown next to it:

- **TRUE**: the claim is correct.
- **FALSE**: the claim is incorrect.
- **UNVERIFIED** (Block B only): the experts could not confirm the claim. Treat it as not
  established.

Take these truth values as given, even if you believe otherwise. Do not look anything up.

An answer is **logically coherent** to the extent that the relations it draws between its
claims **fit their truth values**. For example:

- giving a true claim as a reason for another true claim fits;
- deriving a false claim from a true one does not;
- refuting a false claim fits;
- calling two true claims incompatible does not.

You are **not** judging style, fluency, completeness, helpfulness, or how many true claims the
answer makes. Judge only whether the relations it draws fit the truth values of its claims.

## Block A: which version is more coherent?

Each screen shows a **question**, the **truth values** of the claims in the changed sentences,
and two versions, **A** and **B**, of one answer. Only the sentences that differ are shown,
each with the sentence just before it, in grey, for orientation. Usually one or two places
differ, marked [1], [2]. *(nothing here)* means that a version has no sentence at that place.
The rest of the answer is identical in both; open *Show the rest of the response* if you need
it.

Answer: **which version's argument is more logically coherent?** Choose A, B, or *About
equal*. Then say how sure you are, from 1 to 3.

- Choose *About equal* when the two versions draw the same relations, or relations that fit the
  truth values equally well. This is a real answer, not a fallback.
- The versions may differ by a whole added sentence or by a rewording. Judge the relations
  either way.
- Aim for about 40 seconds per screen.

## Block B1: rate an answer

Each screen shows a question and a natural answer written by an AI system. Every claim is marked
TRUE, FALSE or UNVERIFIED by domain experts. Read the whole answer, then rate **how logically
coherent its argument is**, from 1 to 7:

- **1**: the relations it draws repeatedly clash with the truth values or with each other (for
  example, it builds on false claims, or presents true claims as incompatible);
- **4**: some relations fit the truth values and some clash;
- **7**: every relation it draws fits the truth values.

An answer that draws few relations but gets them right can still rate high. Optionally, select
up to two passages where the argument goes wrong. Aim for about two minutes per answer.

## Block B2: check a relation

You see the same answers again, in the same order. Each screen highlights two of the answer's
claims, **[X]** in blue and **[Y]** in orange, and gives a **proposed reading** of how the answer
relates them.

1. **Does the answer draw a connection between X and Y like the proposed reading?**
   - *Yes*: the text clearly presents this connection.
   - *Partly*: it suggests a connection, but weaker or vaguer than the reading.
   - *No*: it draws no such connection between X and Y.
2. If *Yes* or *Partly*: **which connection does the answer draw?**
   - *Support*: one is given as a reason for the other.
   - *Conflict*: they are presented as unable to hold together.
   - *Neither*: contrast, sequence, or some other connection.

Some readings are wrong on purpose. Judge only what the text says, not whether the reading
would be true. Aim for about 30 seconds per screen.

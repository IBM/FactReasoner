# Annotator brief: comparing responses for logical coherence

Thank you for helping. This should take about **70 minutes** for 14 screens. Please do it in
one sitting if you can, and please read this page fully before starting — it is short, and
the task is easy to do accidentally wrong.

## The task

Each screen shows **two responses, A and B**, answering the same question. You decide which
one is **more logically coherent**, or whether they are about equal.

That is the whole task. There is no taxonomy to learn and nothing to label inside the text.

## What "logically coherent" means here

A response is logically coherent when **the things it says can all be true together, and the
connections it draws between them hold up**.

Ask yourself: *if I believed every claim in this response, would I be believing something
consistent?* Look for:

- **Contradictions** — two statements that cannot both hold. ("The alloy failed the test." …
  "The alloy passed every test.")
- **Connections that do not follow** — a "therefore", "because" or "which shows that" linking
  two things that do not actually support each other.
- **Tensions the text raises and then leaves hanging** — versus tensions it raises and then
  settles, which is *fine* and often a sign of careful writing.
- **Broken chains** — a line of reasoning where a step is missing, so the conclusion no longer
  follows from what came before.

## What is NOT the target

This is the part most easily got wrong, so please read it twice. **Do not reward:**

- **Length.** Longer is not more coherent. Some pairs differ in length by a few words; ignore it.
- **Fluency, style or polish.** Both responses were written to the same standard. If one reads
  slightly more smoothly, that is not what we are measuring.
- **Vocabulary or sophistication.** Technical richness is not coherence.
- **Topic knowledge.** You do **not** need to know whether the claims are true in the real
  world. A response can be perfectly coherent and factually wrong, and that still counts as
  coherent for this task. Judge the *internal* consistency only.
- **Sentence order in itself.** Some pairs contain much the same content in a different order.
  Reordering alone does not make a response less coherent — unless the new order actually
  breaks a connection (for example, a conclusion now appears before the evidence it depends
  on, in a way that genuinely misleads).

That last point matters and is a real question we are trying to answer, so please judge it
honestly rather than assuming a reordered response must be worse or must be equal.

## The three answers

- **A** — A is more logically coherent than B.
- **B** — B is more logically coherent than A.
- **about equal** — you cannot tell them apart on coherence, or any difference you see is
  about style, length or wording rather than consistency.

**"About equal" is a real answer, not a cop-out.** Some pairs genuinely are equivalent, and we
need to know which. Please use it whenever it is honest — but do not use it just to avoid
deciding. If you can see a difference in consistency, however small, pick a side and mark your
confidence as low.

## Confidence

- **low** — a guess; I could easily have gone the other way.
- **medium** — I have a reason but I am not certain.
- **high** — I am confident and could point to the specific problem.

Use **low** freely. A confident-looking dataset full of coin-flips is worse for us than an
honest one that says where the judgement was hard.

## The "why" box

One sentence. Say what actually drove your choice — ideally naming the specific claims or the
specific connection. For example:

- *"B says the committee found no fault but then says the fault was the cause."*
- *"A links the funding cut to the closure with 'therefore' but nothing connects them."*
- *"Same content, different order, no difference in what holds together."*

Short is fine. This box is genuinely useful to us: it lets us check whether readers are
responding to the relational structure or to something else, and it costs you ten seconds.

## Worked examples

**A wins.** A describes a fault, an investigation and a finding that fit together. B describes
the same fault, but also states the investigation found no fault while treating the fault as
established. → **A**, high confidence, *"B asserts the fault and denies it."*

**B wins.** A presents two mutually exclusive explanations and treats both as holding at once.
B presents the same two and says the evidence settles on one. → **B**, high confidence,
*"A leaves an either/or unresolved and then relies on both."*

**About equal.** A and B contain the same claims, but A opens with the conclusion and B ends
with it. Nothing in either is inconsistent. → **about equal**, medium confidence, *"Same
content reordered; both hold together."*

## Practical notes

- Read both responses fully before answering. They are ~650 words each.
- **A and B are in random order** — there is no pattern, and neither side is "usually" right.
- The screens are **not** ordered by difficulty and consecutive screens are unrelated.
- If you notice two screens sharing content, that is expected. Judge each screen on its own.
- There is no time limit per screen, but if you have stared at one for more than five minutes,
  answer "about equal" with low confidence and move on. That is a useful signal too.
- Please do not discuss the screens with the other annotators until everyone has finished. We
  measure how much you independently agree, so a conversation mid-study destroys that number.

## What happens to your answers

Your labels are aggregated with two other annotators' and compared against a coherence measure
we are evaluating. We are testing **our measure**, not you. There is no right answer we are
hoping you produce — in particular, if the three of you disagree on a screen, that is a
publishable finding about the item's ambiguity, not a failure on your part.
